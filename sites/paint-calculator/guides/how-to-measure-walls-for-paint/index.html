Paint measuring guide

How to measure walls for paint

Measure each wall’s width and height, multiply them to find its area, then add the walls together. Subtract openings you will not paint. A ceiling is a separate surface—not part of the wall total.

For a rectangular room with four walls of equal height, you can use room length, width and wall height instead. Have those measurements ready? Use the paint calculator. For a single feature wall, large openings or sloping surfaces, use the manual method below.

1. Measure wall area, not floor area

Use a tape measure and a simple room sketch. Label each wall so you can mark it off as you go. Write the unit beside every measurement; use metres throughout or feet throughout, rather than mixing the two.

  1. Measure the width of each wall from corner to corner along its face.
  2. Measure the height you intend to paint. Usually this is the wall height between floor and ceiling. If you are leaving a band or panelling unpainted, measure only the painted section for a manual calculation.
  3. Multiply width by height. Measurements in metres give square metres (m²); measurements in feet give square feet (ft²).
  4. Add the wall areas. Include each painted face once. For an alcove or projecting chimney breast, list its separate faces rather than treating the whole room as one rectangle.

One rectangular wall: area = width × height
Four equal-height walls: area = 2 × (room length + room width) × wall height

A room’s length × width gives its floor area, not its wall area. The four-wall shortcut works because it first finds the distance around the room, then multiplies that perimeter by the wall height.

Dulux’s measuring guidance uses the same height-times-width method. Sherwin-Williams also recommends measuring and adding the individual walls.

Uneven heights or a sloping top?

Break the painted surface into rectangles and triangles, then add their areas. A triangle’s area is base × perpendicular height ÷ 2. The calculator has one shared wall-height field; it does not model slopes, alcoves or different heights for individual walls.

Using feet and inches

Convert inches to a fraction of a foot before multiplying: feet + inches ÷ 12. For example, 8 ft 6 in is 8.5 ft, not 8.6 ft. In the calculator, choose Imperial for feet and US gallons, or Metric for metres and litres.

Choose your unit system before entering measurements. Switching systems converts the dimensions and coverage already entered, rounding those inputs to two decimal places; it does not simply relabel them. Small estimate changes are possible after switching. Imperial uses US gallons, not UK imperial gallons.

2. Subtract doors and windows you will not paint

Measure the width and height of each opening that replaces painted wall, then subtract width × height from your wall total. Count each opening once. If you are using a different paint for door leaves, frames or trim, keep those surfaces on a separate list; deducting a doorway does not estimate paint for the door itself.

For deep window recesses, add the side, top and sill surfaces back into your manual total if they need the same paint. An opening’s face area does not include these extra surfaces.

The calculator uses fixed opening allowances, not your measured opening sizes. It subtracts 1.9 m² per door and 1.4 m² per window—about 20.5 ft² and 15.1 ft². The starting form includes one door and one window. Set either count to zero if it does not apply.

There is no custom opening-size or direct wall-area field. For a large glazed door, several unusually sized windows or an open archway, use measured deductions and the manual paint formula below. Do not shrink the room dimensions to compensate: that changes the other walls and, if selected, the ceiling too.

Use whole-number opening counts. If deductions exceed the wall area, recheck them rather than trusting the result: the tool clamps its combined wall-and-ceiling area to zero, but does not warn that the opening counts are impossible.

These standard sizes are the tool’s estimating assumptions, not universal door and window dimensions. Dulux advises measuring the openings; Sherwin-Williams specifically highlights large windows and doors. Small deductions may not change the tins you buy, but recording them makes your estimate easier to check.

Measured openings versus standard allowances

A 4 m × 3 m room with 2.4 m walls has 33.6 m² of gross wall area. Suppose its doorway measures 0.9 m × 2.1 m and its window measures 1.5 m × 1.2 m:

  • Doorway: 0.9 × 2.1 = 1.89 m²
  • Window: 1.5 × 1.2 = 1.8 m²
  • Measured net area: 33.6 − 1.89 − 1.8 = 29.91 m²

Entering one door and one window in the calculator instead gives 33.6 − 1.9 − 1.4 = 30.3 m². The difference comes from the opening assumptions, not from a different wall-area formula.

3. Measure the ceiling separately

For a flat rectangular ceiling, multiply room length by room width. A 4 m × 3 m ceiling adds 12 m². Do not subtract wall doors or windows from this ceiling area.

The calculator’s Include the ceiling option is under Fine-tune the estimate and starts switched off. Switching it on adds length × width to the wall estimate. It applies the same coverage, coat count and waste allowance to both surfaces.

Use that combined estimate only when the ceiling and walls use the same paint and assumptions. If the ceiling needs a different product, colour, finish or coat count, leave the option off and calculate ceiling paint separately. The tool has no ceiling-only mode or separate ceiling coverage field.

A vaulted or sloping ceiling has more surface area than its flat footprint. Measure the actual ceiling faces and calculate them separately; the checkbox does not account for their slope.

4. Turn your measurements into a paint estimate

Wall area tells you how much surface you have, not how many litres or gallons to buy. Next choose the number of finish coats and the coverage per coat from your chosen paint’s label or product information. Dulux’s method multiplies area by coats and divides by coverage. A waste allowance is an additional planning choice.

Paint = (paintable area × coats ÷ coverage per coat) × (1 + waste percentage ÷ 100)

Use m² with m²/L, or ft² with ft² per US gallon. Calculate primer separately using its own coverage and coat requirements. The calculator starts with two finish coats, 10 m²/L coverage and 10% waste; those are editable defaults, not promises about a particular paint or surface.

Rectangular room: reproduce this in the calculator

Select Metric. Enter length 4 m, width 3 m, height 2.4 m, 2 coats, 1 door and 1 window. Under Fine-tune the estimate, use 10 m²/L and 10% waste. Leave the ceiling unchecked.

  1. Gross walls: 2 × (4 + 3) × 2.4 = 33.6 m²
  2. Standard opening deductions: 1.9 + 1.4 = 3.3 m²
  3. Paintable walls: 33.6 − 3.3 = 30.3 m²
  4. Two coats before waste: 30.3 × 2 ÷ 10 = 6.06 L
  5. With waste: 6.06 × 1.10 = 6.666 L

The calculator displays 6.67 L and says Plan for 7 L. Its metric purchasing estimate rounds up to the next 0.5 L; its imperial estimate rounds up to the next 0.25 US gallon. These increments are not a check of available tin sizes. Choose actual packs that meet your requirement.

The result labelled Exact estimate is displayed to at most two decimal places, not at full calculation precision. Paint per coat is the area divided by coverage, before waste; here it is 3.03 L. A quantity already on a purchasing increment stays there rather than gaining another increment.

With the same assumptions and Include the ceiling selected, the area becomes 42.3 m². The estimate is 9.306 L, displayed as 9.31 L, with 9.5 L to plan for.

If that 12 m² ceiling instead uses a separate paint at the same assumed coverage, coats and waste, its manual estimate is 12 × 2 ÷ 10 × 1.10 = 2.64 L. Buy wall and ceiling products separately; do not treat their combined volume as interchangeable.

One feature wall: use the manual formula

A 10 ft × 10 ft wall has 100 ft² before openings. At an illustrative 400 ft² per US gallon, two coats and 10% waste require 100 × 2 ÷ 400 × 1.10 = 0.55 US gallon. Check your product’s actual coverage and available pack sizes.

This is a single-wall calculation, not an input recipe for the room calculator. Entering 10 ft as both room length and width would estimate four walls, not one.

Even careful measurements give a planning estimate. Benjamin Moore notes that paint needs vary with factors including application method. Check the product instructions for your surface rather than treating an extra allowance as a substitute for the right coverage rate.

Before you buy: check these five notes

  1. Surfaces: have you counted every painted wall face, without using floor area in place of wall area?
  2. Openings: are you using measured areas or the calculator’s standard deductions? Have you removed the starting door/window counts where needed?
  3. Separate paints: are ceilings, trim, feature colours and primer on their own lists where appropriate?
  4. Coverage and coats: do they match your selected product and surface, rather than the example numbers?
  5. Purchase quantity: have you compared the estimate with real tin sizes? For several rooms using the same paint and assumptions, add unrounded estimates before choosing packs.
